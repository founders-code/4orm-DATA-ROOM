How the Plan Checks Itself

A model that only works one way has not been tested. The workbook compares itself in sixty five places and fails loudly when two parts disagree, and nine on/off switches let a reader turn whole parts of the plan off and watch the numbers move in about a minute. On this plan the single assumption it leans on most is the government research and development tax credits.

Ten pass, two ask for a look

Twelve headline checks sit on their own page. Ten agree to the dollar, and two are marked for review: how much cash is in hand at the 2028 round, and whether any single month runs the account below zero.

The two review items are facts the plan already states

The plan walks into the 2028 round with about one month of costs in hand, and eight months across the road dip below zero. Neither is a mistake in the arithmetic; both are timing facts printed on the face of the model.

Turn a part off and recalculate

Each switch was thrown one at a time and every tab recalculated with no errors. Removing the research and development credits deepens the low point most, to $1,336,747 and sixteen overdrawn months.

Every figure is traced or marked an estimate

Numbers are labelled as read from a named public source, a management estimate, a published benchmark, or the company's own model. Where no published Canadian figure exists, the plan says so rather than reaching for the nearest thing.

Regulatory risk

Rules and Policy Risk

This looks at whether the rules 4orm is built on will hold long enough and behave the way the plan assumes. The company's whole approach depends on rules arriving with a date, changing as written words rather than as software, and staying put. Two claims were dropped from the plan in August 2026 because they could not be backed by a source, and both were about regulation. It matters because if a rule shrinks, or a new duty needs fresh software each time, the revenue and cost assumptions move.

Sell the duty already in force

The daily record rule has applied since 8 September 2025 and the yearly report is fixed at 31 March, so both can be checked today. The three-year review has no first date, so the plan treats it as arithmetic, never a deadline.

A new duty must be writing, not code

The plan claims a new rule can be added by the person who owns the rules signing a written entry, with no software build. If any rule instead needs an engineer, a ticket and a code review, then serving a second regulator means a second build, and the cost case is wrong.

A record year for enforcement

Canada's money-laundering watchdog imposed CAD $203.1M in penalties in 2025 against $19.0M the year before, including the largest penalty in Canadian history. 4orm's defence is that it holds no customer money and signs nothing, so the duty and the risk stay with the customer.

No price on a group with no register

The plan refuses to attach revenue to any group that has no public register of approved firms. That is why consumer-driven banking sits at 578 firms and zero revenue, and universal enrolment carries nothing.

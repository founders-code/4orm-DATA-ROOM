Who owes the duty

Everyone Who Must Prove the Money Is There

This paper maps every kind of Canadian business that holds money belonging to other people and must prove, on a schedule, that the money is all there. There are 18,254 such firms across 31 segments and 11 different regulators, worth about $840.5M a year at list prices once double-counted firms are removed. One software engine can serve all of them because the underlying work is the same each time, only the report format and the deadline change. The plan starts with the smallest and best-counted group: 745 payment firms whose regulator publishes them as a named list.

What you are looking to know

  • Which kinds of business have to keep proving the money is there, and who regulates each
  • How often the duty falls due, from every day down to once a year
  • How firmly each group is counted versus estimated, and why the split is disclosed
  • Why one software build can serve 31 different segments
  • Why the smallest group, payment firms, is the one to sell to first
  • What each new segment actually costs to add after the first

One engine, many clocks

Reading the bank account, matching it against the list of what each customer is owed, keeping a record that cannot be altered afterwards, and holding it for years is the same job whether the money is a payment balance, a legal retainer, a rent deposit or a reserve. Only the report shape and the deadline differ.

Counted or estimated, stated plainly

About 60 per cent of the firms are counted from a regulator's own list; 40 per cent are estimated because their regulator publishes no head count. The paper marks each so a reader can discount the estimates one by one.

Why payments comes first

Payments is the smallest of the five families but the only one whose regulator publishes it as a named list, with a flag on each entry showing which firms hold customer money, and the duty is already in force.

Cheap to extend, cheap to copy

Four of the five layers are reused for each new segment, so the second segment costs a fraction of the first. That same property makes a Canadian version cheap for a British vendor to copy, so the defence is the dated records already built up, not the code.

When a Rule Fails to Create a Market

A rule only creates a market when it is in force, the group of firms it names is countable, and nobody can simply wait it out. This document studies two rules elsewhere that failed one of those tests and took software suppliers down with them, then holds Canada's payments safeguarding rule up to the same checks. The honest finding is that the Canadian rule passes most tests but not all: it sets no fixed date for a first independent review, so the plan cannot lean on a deadline.

Two rules that did not pay off

In Europe, a sustainability reporting rule was cut from about 50,000 companies to 5,000 to 6,000, and one supplier built against it shut down all operations. In the United Kingdom, a digital tax plan was delayed five times, and suppliers who had staffed up for it wrote the cost off.

Where Canada's rule stands

The Canadian daily duty has applied since 8 September 2025, the firms it covers are named on a public register, and enforcement has already started. On the five tests, it clears three cleanly and is strong on a fourth.

The test it fails

The rule requires an independent review at least once every three years but names no first date. So the urgency cannot rest on a deadline. It rests instead on a duty that falls due every single day and has already fallen due hundreds of times.

What the document does not claim

It does not claim that regulation guarantees a market, that the overseas cases prove anything about Canada, or that any Canadian regulator has endorsed the company or its product.
